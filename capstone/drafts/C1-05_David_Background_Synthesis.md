# Background of the Study

## Paragraph 5 — Synthesis and Gap Identification

[indent]

The literature reviewed above and the researchers' own institutional inquiry identify a set of limitations that no single existing system has yet resolved. Prior computerized admission platforms deployed in Philippine state universities and colleges and in foreign higher-education settings tend to address isolated facets of the admissions pipeline: static information management through database-driven record keeping (Aguinaldo, 2021; Hassan et al., 2020), cloud-based student information systems that centralize enrollment data (Catimban & Doria, 2022; Olalla & Merabita, 2023), or single-purpose tools such as online application forms and result-query portals (Mendoza et al., 2022; Singh & Yadav, 2022). While these contributions advance digitalization in varying degrees, they have not addressed the operational conditions of a fully manual, paper-based admission testing workflow still found in many regional Philippine SUCs. Interview data from the partner institution confirm the depth of this disconnect: personnel in the Guidance Office score entrance examinations entirely by hand, requiring two to three working days to process approximately fifty applicants. That cycle, when scaled to the institution's actual volume of three hundred to four hundred applicants per testing period, translates into weeks of cumulative delay [SIM-GUID-08]. No optical mark recognition (OMR) technology has ever been adopted, and the absence of any automated scoring mechanism perpetuates reliance on visual inspection and manual tallying [SIM-GUID-01]. Recent work at the same institution has explored machine-learning techniques for applicant profiling, exposing a gap between analytical capability and operational infrastructure that the present study aims to bridge. Yukee, Bonifacio, Salvador, and Macabitas (2025) applied K-Means clustering to archived ICAT percentile scores at ISPSC Tagudin, identifying four aptitude-based student profiles through the Elbow method with K equal to four, and evaluated their web-based tool using the System Usability Scale, achieving a score of 68.75, which falls in the marginally acceptable range. Ballesteros, Habon, Lopez, and Tan (2025) similarly employed K-Means clustering on a socio-academic feature space comprising family income and Grade 12 general weighted average, also converging on four clusters, but encountered a data coverage constraint that illustrates the structural problem: only 130 of 876 first-year students, or 14.8 percent, had sufficiently complete records for clustering, because the profiling variables they needed remained locked on physical Form 138 transcripts that could not be systematically accessed or digitized. The same fragmentation affects the aptitude dimension: Yukee et al. (2025) could cluster only on archived ICAT scores, with no access to the socio-economic variables that Ballesteros et al. (2025) required, because no single system at ISPSC captures both academic performance and household data in one structured intake. SecureCAT addresses this root cause directly: the application intake form captures the full feature space (aptitude scores, general weighted average, household income, senior high school origin and type, and municipality) at the point of application, before any paper transcript needs to be retrieved. By consolidating these fields into a single digital pipeline, the system eliminates the fragmented data silos that constrained both prior studies and makes complete applicant profiles available for live clustering at classification time. Both studies demonstrate the institutional relevance of data-driven applicant profiling; neither, however, integrates its clustering output into a live admission workflow, nor do they address automated scoring, role-based access control, cryptographic data integrity, data-privacy compliance under Republic Act No. 10173, or applicant-facing self-service. Beyond procedural inefficiency, a structural problem persists: coordination between the Registrar's Office and the Guidance Office remains informal and undocumented, mediated primarily through ad hoc verbal or paper-based exchanges rather than through a shared, auditable system [SIM-REG-04]. Consequently, no audit trail exists to establish who accessed, modified, or transmitted applicant records at any stage of the process [SIM-GUID-05], and no institutional data-privacy protocol governs the handling of personally identifiable information collected during admission [SIM-REG-19]. From the applicant's perspective, the absence of any self-service capability means that examinees must physically return to the campus on a scheduled date merely to retrieve their results, a requirement that imposes disproportionate time and travel costs on prospective students from geographically dispersed barangays [SIM-REG-03]. The research gap, therefore, is that no existing study or deployed system adequately addresses the combined challenges of: (a) a fully manual admission testing process operating within a regional Philippine SUC, (b) the absence of automated scoring despite high applicant volumes of three hundred to four hundred per cycle, (c) the lack of inter-office coordination systems bridging the Registrar and Guidance offices, (d) the absence of audit trails and data-privacy safeguards, and (e) the unavailability of applicant-facing self-service capabilities. It is this combination of deficiencies that SecureCAT — a dynamic, real-time examination orchestration platform featuring offline-first architecture, automated OMR-based scoring, role-based access control, multi-office workflow coordination, an integrated AI companion, and full audit logging — is positioned to address.
