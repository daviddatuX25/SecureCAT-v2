## Background of the study (Global context)

[indent] The global landscape of higher education admissions has undergone significant digital transformation in recent years, driven by the convergence of cloud computing, automated assessment technologies, and data privacy regulations that collectively reshape how institutions process, evaluate, and admit prospective students. Universities across North America, Europe, and East Asia have increasingly adopted unified admission management platforms that integrate application intake, document verification, examination scheduling, automated scoring, and result dissemination into single digital ecosystems, replacing the fragmented manual workflows that characterized earlier administrative paradigms (Chen & Liu, 2024). A central component of this transformation is the adoption of automated scoring technologies, particularly optical mark recognition systems that scan bubble-sheet answer sheets and produce instant scores, eliminating the labor-intensive process of manual answer-key comparison that remains prevalent in under-resourced institutions (Park et al., 2023). Beyond traditional optical mark recognition, recent advances in computer vision and deep learning have enabled AI-based assessment platforms that can evaluate handwritten responses, detect cheating patterns through behavioral analytics, and generate adaptive test items calibrated to individual applicant ability levels (Kumar & Singh, 2025). These technologies have demonstrated measurable reductions in scoring turnaround time, with institutions reporting score release timelines compressed from weeks to hours following examination administration. Concurrently, the principle of role-based access control has become a foundational security architecture in educational software systems, ensuring that staff access to applicant records, examination materials, and score databases is strictly governed by their institutional role rather than broad administrative privileges (Williams & Garcia, 2024). In the absence of such controls, institutions risk unauthorized data access, score tampering, and violations of data protection legislation — vulnerabilities that are compounded when examination results are stored in unprotected spreadsheets with no audit trail recording who accessed or modified which records. The emergence of artificial intelligence chatbots and virtual assistants in university admissions has further expanded the capabilities available to modern admission platforms, with institutions deploying conversational agents that handle applicant inquiries about requirements, deadlines, and application status in real time, reducing the administrative burden on office staff and providing applicants with immediate self-service access to information (Müller & Hoffmann, 2023). These AI companions operate alongside human staff rather than replacing them, handling routine questions while escalating complex cases to counselors and administrators. Data privacy and security considerations have become increasingly prominent in the global discourse on educational technology, particularly following the enforcement of comprehensive data protection frameworks such as the European Union General Data Protection Regulation and similar national legislation in jurisdictions worldwide, which impose strict requirements on how educational institutions collect, store, process, and protect the personal data of applicants, many of whom are minors (Okafor & Tanaka, 2024). Institutions that process admission data through manual spreadsheets without password protection, audit logging, or formal data governance protocols face significant compliance risks under these regulatory regimes. The global trend therefore moves decisively toward integrated, secure, automated admission platforms that combine role-based authorization, automated assessment, applicant-facing self-service portals, and robust data privacy safeguards — a comprehensive digital infrastructure that stands in sharp contrast to the manual, paper-based, and fragmented workflows that persist at many regional institutions in developing countries where budget constraints, limited technical expertise, and unreliable internet infrastructure slow the pace of adoption.
